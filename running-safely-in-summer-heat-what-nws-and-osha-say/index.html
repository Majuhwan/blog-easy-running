Running Safely in Summer Heat, What NWS and OSHA Say

A guide to reading the heat index and spotting high-risk conditions for outdoor running, based on National Weather Service and OSHA heat safety guidance.

Easy RunningOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

A hot morning run can feel fine for the first mile and turn dangerous by the third, because the number most runners check (the air temperature) is not the number that actually predicts heat stress. Two federal sources, the National Weather Service (NWS) and the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), explain what actually drives heat risk during exercise and why the plain temperature reading is often misleading.

The heat index is not the same as the temperature

The NWS defines the heat index as "a measure of how hot it really feels when relative humidity is factored in with the actual air temperature." Its own example makes the gap clear: at an air temperature of 96°F with 65% relative humidity, the heat index, how hot it actually feels, is 121°F. That is a 25-degree difference between what the thermometer shows and what your body experiences, driven entirely by humidity.

The NWS also flags two things the standard heat index number does not include. First, heat index values are calculated for "shady, light wind conditions," so running in direct sunlight can push the real feel up by as much as 15°F higher than the posted heat index. Second, strong winds combined with very hot, dry air can be hazardous in a way the heat index does not capture either. Both of these point the same direction for runners: a posted heat index is a floor, not a ceiling, on how intense conditions actually feel once you are moving in full sun.

Why the heat index still is not the full picture for exercise

OSHA's heat hazard guidance goes a step further than NWS and identifies a more complete measurement called Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT), which factors in temperature, humidity, wind, and radiant heat (heat from direct sunlight) together. OSHA states plainly: "The heat index does not account for the effects of wind, sunlight, radiant heat sources, or workload," and recommends WBGT instead of heat index wherever it is available, noting that "the American College of Sports Medicine bases its guidelines for the intensity of sport practices on WBGT."

OSHA's guidance was written primarily for outdoor workers, but the physiology is the same for anyone exercising outside: your body generates heat from the activity itself (what OSHA calls "metabolic heat"), and that heat adds to whatever the environment is already producing. OSHA's workload table rates "fast walking (greater than 4 mph)" as heavy work and anything close to maximum pace as very heavy work, both of which it flags as carrying the highest heat-illness risk. Running at almost any pace falls solidly into those two categories.

One number from OSHA's guidance is especially useful for runners who think heat illness only happens in extreme conditions: "Outdoor workers have died of heat stroke when the day's maximum Heat Index was only 86°F." That figure is a reminder that the risk threshold for a body working hard outdoors is lower than it feels like it should be, especially for someone who is not yet acclimated to the heat.

What acclimatization changes

OSHA's guidance distinguishes between unacclimatized workers (generally those who have been in the conditions for less than one to two weeks) and acclimatized ones, and the two groups have different safe thresholds. For workers doing heavy exertion, OSHA's simplified recommendations put the unacclimatized action limit at an effective WBGT of 23°C (73.4°F), compared with 26°C (78.8°F) for someone already acclimatized to the heat. In plain terms: the first week or two of running through a heat wave, or the first runs after returning from a cooler trip, carry meaningfully higher risk than the same conditions later in the season once your body has adjusted.

A practical checklist before a hot-weather run

Based directly on what these two sources measure and recommend:

  • Check the heat index, not just the temperature, using the NWS heat index chart or calculator before you head out.
  • Add a mental buffer for direct sun. NWS notes sun exposure alone can raise the real feel by up to 15°F above the posted heat index.
  • Treat an 86°F heat index as a real risk threshold, not just the triple-digit numbers; OSHA's own data shows fatal heat stroke has occurred at that level under heavy exertion.
  • Be more cautious in your first one to two weeks of hot weather, since OSHA's safe thresholds for unacclimatized people are measurably lower than for people used to the heat.
  • Reduce pace or shorten the run on high heat-index days, since OSHA explicitly rates faster running paces in its highest-risk workload categories.

Key takeaways

  • The heat index, not plain air temperature, is the number that should guide a decision to run outside; NWS's own example shows a 25-degree gap between the two at 96°F and 65% humidity.
  • Direct sunlight and workload both push real heat stress higher than the posted heat index accounts for, per both NWS and OSHA.
  • OSHA's data shows fatal heat stroke has occurred at a heat index of only 86°F under heavy exertion, a useful lower bound for taking heat seriously.
  • The first one to two weeks of running in new heat carry higher risk than later weeks, because OSHA's safe exposure limits are measurably lower for unacclimatized people.
  • Faster paces and harder efforts raise your personal heat-generation rate on top of whatever the environment is already producing, so slowing down is a legitimate heat-safety strategy, not just a performance choice.
