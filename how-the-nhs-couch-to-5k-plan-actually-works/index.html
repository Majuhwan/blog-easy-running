How the NHS Couch to 5K Plan Actually Works

A breakdown of the NHS Couch to 5K structure week by week, showing exactly how the run and walk intervals build toward a continuous 30-minute run.

Easy RunningOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

This article is informational only and is not medical advice. Speak to a doctor before starting a new running program, especially if you have health concerns.

Couch to 5K is a free, structured running plan published by the NHS, built around a simple idea: alternate walking and running in a pattern that gradually shifts toward more running, over 9 weeks, 3 runs a week. What makes the plan useful for complete beginners isn't just that it exists, but how precisely it's structured. Here is exactly how the intervals progress, according to the NHS's own published plan.

The starting point: Week 1

Every run in Week 1 follows the same structure: a 5-minute warm-up walk, then a pattern of running for 1 minute and walking for 1 minute 30 seconds, repeated 7 times in total, followed by a final 1-minute run and a 5-minute cool-down walk. Total time per run, excluding stretches, is 28 minutes 30 seconds. All three runs in Week 1 are identical, which means you repeat the exact same session three times before the plan changes anything.

How the running time grows

Rather than jumping straight from short intervals to long continuous runs, the NHS plan increases gradually, and the structure shifts in recognizable stages:

  • Week 2: Run 1 minute 30 seconds, walk 2 minutes, repeated 5 times, then a final 1 minute 30 second run. Total time: 29 minutes.
  • Week 3: A mixed pattern of 1 minute 30 second and 3-minute runs, with walking breaks in between, ending in a final 3-minute run. Total time: 25 minutes.
  • Week 4: Runs stretch to 3 and 5 minutes within the same session. Total time: 31 minutes 30 seconds.
  • Week 5: This week includes three different run structures across its three sessions, culminating in Run 3, which is a single continuous 20-minute run, the plan's first major milestone.

The jump to a 20-minute continuous run in Week 5, Run 3, is a significant shift from the interval-based sessions before it, and the NHS plan explicitly frames it as a milestone worth noting.

Why the plan goes backward in Week 6

After reaching 20 minutes of continuous running in Week 5, you might expect Week 6 to immediately push further. Instead, the NHS plan returns to interval-style running for Week 6's first two sessions, mixing runs of 5, 8, and 10 minutes with walking breaks, before building to a continuous 25-minute run in Week 6's third session.

This is a deliberate design choice rather than a mistake in the plan. Giving your body a chance to adjust to the new continuous-running demand, rather than stacking another big jump immediately on top of the previous one, is a recognizable pattern throughout the plan: build to a new milestone, then consolidate before pushing further.

The final stretch: Weeks 7 through 9

From Week 7 onward, the structure simplifies considerably:

  • Week 7: All three runs are a continuous 25 minutes.
  • Week 8: All three runs increase to a continuous 28 minutes.
  • Week 9: All three runs reach a continuous 30 minutes, the plan's final target.

By the structure of these final three weeks, the plan shifts from interval training entirely to repeated continuous runs at increasing duration, which mirrors how training for longer continuous efforts typically works: once your body can sustain a given duration, the next step is simply extending that same duration slightly.

Built-in rest and recovery

Every single run, in every week, starts with a 5-minute warm-up walk and ends with a 5-minute cool-down walk, which are counted in the total session time but separate from the running and walking intervals themselves. The NHS plan also recommends a rest day between each of the 3 weekly runs, giving your body a full day between sessions throughout the entire 9-week program, not just in the early weeks.

What this structure tells you about building running endurance

Looking at the plan as a whole, a few patterns repeat across all 9 weeks: duration increases happen gradually rather than in large jumps, each new structure is typically repeated multiple times within the same week before moving on, and periods of consolidation, like Week 6's return to intervals, follow major milestones rather than preceding them. If you are building your own approach to increasing running duration, even outside this specific plan, these same principles, gradual increases, repetition before progression, and consolidation after milestones, are worth keeping in mind.

How this fits general activity guidance

The World Health Organization's physical activity fact sheet recommends that adults get 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week. By Week 7 of Couch to 5K, each 25-minute continuous run, 3 times a week, already totals 75 minutes, and that is before counting the 5-minute warm-up and cool-down walks included in every session. This shows how a structured beginner plan like Couch to 5K can move you toward, and eventually past, general weekly activity guidance without you needing to calculate it separately.

Key takeaways

  • Couch to 5K runs 3 times a week for 9 weeks, with every session including a 5-minute warm-up and cool-down walk.
  • Week 1 starts with repeated 1-minute runs and 1-minute-30-second walks, totaling 28 minutes 30 seconds per session.
  • Week 5's third run is the plan's first continuous 20-minute run, a clear milestone in the structure.
  • Week 6 deliberately returns to interval-style running before building to a continuous 25-minute run, rather than pushing straight past the Week 5 milestone.
  • Weeks 7 through 9 consist of continuous runs of 25, 28, and 30 minutes respectively, with no further interval work.
